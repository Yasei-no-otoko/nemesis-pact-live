## Project description

NEMESIS PACT is a browser roguelite arena shooter where negotiating with an enemy changes the rules you must survive. Speak or type to THE NOTARY, inspect the benefits and costs of a Living Covenant, and explicitly sign before it affects combat. A sanctuary can erase bullets while allowing hostile reinforcements; stronger reflections can cost ordinary firepower. First Contact offers a short encounter with one mid-fight renegotiation and recorded consequences for each signed revision. The full campaign spans six sectors, distinct bosses, three airframes, branching routes, upgrades, and relics. Optional GPT-5.6 Luna analysis reviews aggregate encounter results and suggests bounded, authored difficulty adjustments. GPT-Live-1 supports voice negotiation, including interruption and correction. English and Japanese interfaces, touch controls, captions, and explicitly labeled LOCAL RULES keep the game playable without AI access. The model negotiates and advises; deterministic game logic remains authoritative.

## Judging criteria 1 (30%) - Meaningful use of OpenAI tools

OpenAI tools affect playable decisions, not just dialogue decoration. GPT-Live-1 supports spoken negotiation with THE NOTARY. Typed and spoken requests feed a bounded covenant pipeline: Luna proposes authored clauses, validation rejects unsupported mechanics, revision tracking invalidates superseded proposals, and only the player's explicit Sign applies the displayed terms. Voice agreement alone cannot sign. Optional campaign analysis uses aggregate wave or sector telemetry to suggest one authored ease, hold, or raise step at encounter boundaries. Bullet speed and arrival spacing changes are capped at plus or minus 16 percent, without rewriting canonical pacts. Unavailable analysis holds the current level with a LOCAL RULES label. The earlier recorded production campaign logged six signed Luna pacts, six Live voice calls, and 18 Luna analyses. Its player speech was synthetic Windows Zira audio sent to the real Live API; it is not human microphone evidence. Codex assisted implementation, testing, and iteration. The final readiness audit used LOCAL RULES and made no new live-model calls.

## Judging criteria 2 (25%) - Originality

The boss is both an adversary and a counterparty: conversation creates a ruleset the player must physically execute. Asking for safety introduces an inspectable cost rather than an unbounded promise. Players can interrupt, revise, compare clauses, and decide whether to sign. First Contact then turns combat evidence into the next decision: remaining hull, boss health, erased bullets, and reflected damage inform one mid-fight amendment. Results separate effects by signed revision, and breaking a pact stops attributing later actions to its discarded terms. Retrying opens an unsigned negotiation rather than silently reusing consent. The six-sector campaign complements this contract loop with optional bounded adaptation between encounters. These systems keep the tension between a persuasive opponent and a mechanically enforceable agreement. The demonstration's fixed autoplay policy supplies repeatable inputs; it is not an AI pilot or evidence that the model controls the player.

## Judging criteria 3 (25%) - Playability / Utility

The public HTTPS game supports desktop and mobile controls, with First Contact as its short entry route and a six-sector campaign for longer play. Auto aim and shooting let newcomers concentrate on movement, dashing, parrying, and their signed terms. English and Japanese copy, captions, transcripts, volume, mute, and explicit consent controls support access without requiring speech. Missing consent opens a choice dialog; LOCAL RULES remains an explicit alternative when AI is unavailable. Combat guidance prioritizes laser danger and accounts for defensive cooldowns. Recorded results explain contract effects, and retry requires a new signature. The final anonymous desktop-browser check reached a local proposal, explicit signature, active combat, and a parley showing actual damage and erased bullets. This was automated browser interaction, not human play, a physical-phone test, or live AI verification. Earlier campaign evidence separately records six bosses and 18 encounters; synthetic mobile-layout checks are not presented as device testing.
